# How to Verify Business Licenses and Federal Permits

Before hiring a contractor, wiring money to an investment advisor, or signing with a moving company, you can check whether that company actually holds the credentials it claims. Nearly every regulated industry in the United States leaves a paper trail in public databases: state registrations, federal permits, disciplinary records, and enforcement actions. To [verify business](https://netstate.co/) licenses and federal permits, you rarely need more than a company name, a state, and fifteen minutes. This guide walks through where those records live, how to read them, and which details separate a legitimate operator from a company running without authorization.

## Why Verification Matters

Unlicensed operators cluster in industries where the stakes are highest. Home improvement fraud consistently ranks among the top consumer complaints reported to state attorneys general, and a large share of those cases involve contractors working without an active license. An unlicensed contractor typically carries no surety bond and no liability insurance, which means a homeowner absorbs the full cost when a roof job fails or a worker is injured on the property. In California, hiring an unlicensed contractor for work over $1,000 can also expose the homeowner to workers' compensation liability.

The same logic applies to money. A person selling securities without registration with the SEC or a state regulator has already broken the law before taking a single dollar. Investment fraud cases frequently begin with a credential that was never checked. The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority operates BrokerCheck precisely because the verification takes two minutes and the losses from skipping it routinely run into six figures.

Federal permits carry a separate weight. A trucking company hauling freight across state lines without operating authority from the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration is uninsured for that cargo in any practical sense. A firearms dealer without a Federal Firearms License commits a felony with each sale. Checking these records protects the customer, but it also protects anyone doing business with the company — a shipper, a landlord, a lender — from becoming entangled in the violation.

## Start at the State Level

### Secretary of State Business Registries

Every U.S. state maintains a business entity database, usually through the Secretary of State's office. This is the foundational record: it confirms the company legally exists, when it was formed, whether it remains in good standing, and who its registered agent is. Search by exact legal name first, then by partial name, because many companies operate under a trade name (a DBA, "doing business as") that differs from the registered entity.

Pay attention to three fields. **Status** should read "Active" or "Good Standing" — entries marked "Dissolved," "Suspended," "Forfeited," or "Administratively Dissolved" mean the entity has lost its legal footing, often for failing to file annual reports or pay franchise taxes. **Formation date** matters when a company claims "20 years in business" but registered eight months ago. **Registered agent address** that points to a residential apartment or a virtual office is not disqualifying by itself, but it belongs on your list of things to reconcile with other claims the company makes.

A state registration is not a license. It is a corporate filing. A registered LLC can still lack every permit its industry requires, so treat the Secretary of State record as step one, never as the finish line.

### State Licensing Boards

Occupational and industry licenses live with specialized state boards: contractor licensing boards, real estate commissions, boards of pharmacy, cosmetology boards, departments of insurance. Most publish free online lookup tools. California's Contractors State License Board, for example, shows the license classification (a C-10 electrical license does not authorize plumbing work), bond amount, workers' compensation coverage, and any disciplinary history. Florida's DBPR portal covers dozens of professions in a single search. New York splits licensing across the Department of State, the Department of Financial Services, and city agencies, so location determines which portal applies.

Match the license to the work being performed. A general contractor license does not cover asbestos abatement in most states; that requires a separate certification. An insurance agent licensed for property and casualty cannot legally sell annuities without a life license. The classification field on the license record answers this directly.

## Federal Permits by Industry

Federal permits apply when a business touches interstate commerce, controlled goods, public airwaves, or federally regulated markets. Each agency runs its own public database, and most require no account to search.

| Industry | Regulator | Public Database | What It Shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trucking, moving, buses | FMCSA | SAFER / L&I System | Operating authority, USDOT number, insurance on file, safety rating, crash history |
| Firearms dealers | ATF | FFL eZ Check | Active Federal Firearms License by FFL number |
| Alcohol producers, importers | TTB | Public COLA Registry / Permits Online | Federal basic permits, label approvals |
| Broker-dealers, advisors | SEC / FINRA | BrokerCheck, IAPD | Registration, employment history, customer disputes, sanctions |
| Mortgage lenders, loan officers | State regulators via NMLS | NMLS Consumer Access | License status in each state, regulatory actions |
| Radio, telecom, broadcast | FCC | Universal Licensing System (ULS) | Spectrum licenses, call signs, expiration dates |
| Aircraft operators | FAA | Airmen and Aircraft Registries | Certificates, aircraft registration, air carrier status |
| Drug manufacturers, food facilities | FDA | Registration & Listing databases | Facility registration, drug establishment status |
| Pharmacies handling controlled substances | DEA | DEA registration validation | Active DEA registration number |
| Federal contractors | GSA | SAM.gov | Registration, exclusion and debarment records |

Two details trip people up. First, a USDOT number alone does not authorize a moving company to transport household goods for hire; that requires separate MC (motor carrier) operating authority, and the FMCSA record distinguishes between the two. Second, FDA "registration" of a facility is not FDA "approval" of a product — supplement companies routinely blur that line in marketing, and the database only confirms the facility filed its registration.

For companies selling to the federal government, SAM.gov serves double duty. It confirms the registration a contractor needs to receive federal awards, and its exclusions section lists parties debarred or suspended from federal contracting. A company appearing on the exclusion list has usually done something worth knowing about, even if you are a private customer.

## Worked Example: Verifying an Interstate Moving Company

Moving fraud follows a known pattern: a low quote by phone, a truck that loads your belongings, then a demand for triple the price before delivery. Every step of prevention runs through public records. Here is the full check, in order:

1. **Get the exact company name and USDOT number.** Federal rules require interstate movers to display the USDOT number on advertising and trucks. Refusal to provide it ends the conversation.
2. **Search FMCSA's mover lookup or SAFER system by USDOT number.** Confirm the entity name matches the name on your estimate. Brokers posing as carriers is a common scheme — the record states whether the company is a CARRIER, a BROKER, or both.
3. **Check operating authority status.** It must show active authority for household goods. "Authorized for Property" is not the same as household goods authority.
4. **Verify insurance on file.** The FMCSA Licensing & Insurance system shows the cargo and liability policies filed with the agency, including the insurer's name and policy expiration.
5. **Review the complaint and safety history.** FMCSA publishes complaint counts and safety data per carrier. A small mover with dozens of hostage-load complaints tells you everything.
6. **Cross-check the state registration.** Look up the entity in the Secretary of State database of its home state. A carrier claiming a decade of experience but formed six weeks ago has likely reincarnated after losing its previous authority — a practice FMCSA calls a "chameleon carrier."

Total time: roughly ten minutes. Cost: zero. The same skeleton — federal database, insurance filing, complaint history, state registration — adapts to almost any regulated industry.

## Reading Regulatory and Enforcement Records

License status answers whether a company *may* operate. Enforcement records answer how it *has* operated. Several databases aggregate this history:

- **EPA ECHO** (Enforcement and Compliance History Online) shows inspections, violations, and penalties for facilities under the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, and RCRA. Useful for evaluating industrial neighbors, waste haulers, and dry cleaners.
- **OSHA's establishment search** lists workplace inspections, citations, and penalty amounts by company name. Repeated "willful" or "repeat" violations indicate a pattern, not an accident.
- **CFPB complaint database** logs consumer complaints against financial companies, with the company's response. Volume matters less than resolution rate for a large bank; for a small lender, raw volume matters a great deal.
- **State attorney general press releases and court records** capture consent decrees and judgments that never appear on a license record. PACER covers federal court cases; most state courts offer name-based searches.
- **FDA warning letters** are published in full and name specific violations at specific facilities, from unsanitary conditions to unapproved drug claims.

Context governs interpretation. A single OSHA citation from 2015 with a $4,000 penalty at a construction firm is background noise. Three willful violations in two years, or a license that was suspended and reinstated twice, is a trajectory. Disciplinary records on professional licenses usually specify the conduct — read the actual order when the board publishes it, because "discipline" spans everything from late continuing-education credits to patient harm.

| State licensing board portals | Free | Occupational licenses, bonds, discipline |
| FMCSA SAFER / mover search | Free | Trucking and moving authority, insurance |

Commercial services such as Dun & Bradstreet or LexisNexis compile some of this data, but for a consumer checking one company, the primary government sources are more current and cost nothing.

## Edge Cases and Local Layers

Verification gets harder at the margins. Several situations deserve specific handling.

**Trade names hide the registered entity.** "Sunrise Plumbing" may be the DBA of "JKL Holdings LLC." DBA filings sit at the county clerk or, in some states, with the Secretary of State. If a license search under the trade name returns nothing, find the DBA filing first, then search under the legal entity name.

**Licenses do not cross state lines automatically.** A contractor licensed in Nevada holds nothing in Arizona unless Arizona granted reciprocity or a separate license. Nurse licensing has an interstate compact; contractor licensing mostly does not. Always check the license in the state where the work occurs.

**Some states barely license contractors at all.** Handyman and general contracting work is unlicensed or lightly regulated in states like Texas (no statewide general contractor license) — there, the verification shifts to city registration, insurance certificates, and bonding, requested directly from the company and confirmed with the insurer.

**Exemptions exist and companies invoke them, sometimes incorrectly.** Small private placements can be exempt from securities registration; intrastate-only truckers may not need federal authority; farms selling certain raw products may skip food facility registration. When a company claims an exemption, the claim itself is checkable — the exemption's conditions are published, and a company relying on a securities exemption should still appear in SEC EDGAR via a Form D filing in most cases.

**License numbers can be borrowed or fabricated.** Matching the number is not enough; match the *name and address* on the license record to the company in front of you. A real license number attached to the wrong business is a documented fraud pattern in contracting and in firearms sales, which is why ATF's FFL eZ Check exists — it lets a seller confirm that an FFL number corresponds to the named licensee before shipping a firearm.

**Recent actions lag the databases.** A license suspended yesterday may show as active today. For high-stakes transactions, call the licensing board and ask whether any action is pending; boards will usually confirm pending discipline even when it is not yet posted.

## What to Do When Verification Fails

A failed check has gradations. A lapsed local business tax receipt is a paperwork problem; ask the company to fix it. A suspended contractor license, a missing MC authority, or an unregistered securities seller is a stop sign — walk away and file a report. Contractor issues go to the state licensing board and the state attorney general's consumer protection division. Moving company fraud goes to FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database. Unregistered securities activity goes to the SEC's tip portal and the state securities regulator. Reports feed the same public databases the next person will search, which is the entire mechanism by which these records stay useful.

Keep your evidence: screenshots of the license lookup with the date visible, the written estimate, and any advertising that displayed the license or DOT number. Regulators act faster on documented complaints, and if the dispute reaches small claims court, the screenshot showing an inactive license on the contract date is often decisive.

## FAQs

### Is a business registration the same as a business license?

No. Registration with the Secretary of State creates the legal entity — the LLC or corporation. A license is a separate authorization to perform regulated activity, issued by a licensing board, agency, or municipality. A company can hold one without the other, so both need checking.

### How do I verify a license if I only know the company's trade name?

Search the county clerk or Secretary of State for the DBA filing, which names the underlying legal entity. Then run license and registration searches under that legal name. If no DBA filing exists anywhere, the company is operating under an unregistered name, which is itself a compliance failure in most states.

### Are these government databases free to use?

Almost all of them. Secretary of State searches, state licensing portals, FMCSA, BrokerCheck, NMLS Consumer Access, SAM.gov, EPA ECHO, and OSHA search cost nothing. PACER charges a small per-page fee for federal court documents, and some county recorders charge for copies of filings, though viewing the index is typically free.

### What does an "inactive" or "delinquent" license status mean?

It varies by board. "Inactive" often means the holder voluntarily paused the license — legal, but the person cannot practice under it. "Delinquent" usually means renewal fees or continuing education lapsed. "Suspended" and "revoked" indicate disciplinary action. None of these statuses authorize current work; only "active" or "current" does.

### Can a company legally operate while its federal permit application is pending?

Generally no, with narrow exceptions. Interstate carriers cannot haul for hire before FMCSA grants authority, and there is a mandatory protest period after grant before the authority becomes effective. Firearms dealers cannot sell before the FFL issues. When a company says its permit is "in process," treat it as unlicensed until the record shows otherwise.

### How current are the online records?

State entity databases usually update within a day or two of filings. License status updates depend on the board — some post disciplinary actions within days, others take weeks. Insurance filings in FMCSA's system update when insurers file, which is near-real-time. For anything decided in the last two weeks, confirm by phone with the issuing agency.

## Conclusion

Every regulated company leaves a verifiable trail: an entity record at the Secretary of State, a license at a state board, and — where interstate commerce or controlled goods are involved — a federal permit in an agency database. The check follows the same sequence regardless of industry. Confirm the legal entity exists and stands in good order. Match the license classification to the actual work, and match the name on the license to the company in front of you. Pull the federal record where one applies, including the insurance filings behind it. Then read the enforcement history, because a valid license with three suspensions tells a different story than the license status alone. The tools are free, the searches take minutes, and the companies most eager to skip this scrutiny are exactly the ones the databases were built to expose.
